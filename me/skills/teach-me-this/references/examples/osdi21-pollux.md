# Pollux: Co-adaptive Cluster Scheduling for Goodput-Optimized Deep Learning
OSDI'21 (Best Paper) · Aurick Qiao, Sang Keun Choe, Suhas Jayaram Subramanya, Willie Neiswanger, Qirong Ho, Hao Zhang, Gregory R. Ganger, Eric P. Xing / Petuum, CMU, UC Berkeley, MBZUAI · https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi21/presentation/qiao
Paper type: new system (cluster scheduler for deep learning training)

## Dissection
- Background: Shared GPU clusters run many deep learning (DL) training jobs at once. Most jobs use synchronous data parallelism: each GPU computes a gradient on its share of the mini-batch, then all GPUs average the gradients (all-reduce) before each step. Iteration time is roughly compute time (Tgrad, shrinks with a smaller per-GPU batch) plus sync time (Tsync, does not shrink with batch size and grows when a job spans nodes). When the total batch grows, the learning rate (LR) must be scaled up by a rule (linear, square-root, AdaScale), or final accuracy suffers. Schedulers fall in two groups. Tiresias and Gandiva take the GPU count from the user and keep it fixed. Optimus, Gavel, AntMan and similar systems pick the GPU count from a model of each job's throughput.
- Problem: Users must choose GPU count, batch size and LR at submission. The three depend on each other: more GPUs only pay off with a larger batch, and a larger batch makes less training progress per example. The best choice also changes while the job runs, with cluster load and with the stage of training. Users choose badly, and even an expert choice is a static value. Schedulers that pick GPU counts look only at throughput and leave the batch alone. So no part of the system chooses resources and training parameters together, using a measure of real training progress.
- Challenges: (1) Progress per example at a batch size never tried is unknown, and final accuracy is known only at the end. Trying each batch size costs full training runs. (2) A throughput model must cover allocations the job has never run on, and it must be fitted online for models the scheduler has never seen. (3) What an allocation is worth to a job depends on how the job will re-tune its batch once it has the GPUs. So the scheduler and the job cannot decide separately. (4) Jobs measure progress in different units and want different amounts of GPUs, so the cluster-wide objective must be comparable across jobs and fair. (5) Each reallocation is a checkpoint-restart that costs 15–120 s. Two distributed jobs on one node slow each other down.
- Solution:
  1. Insight: how much progress per example a larger batch loses is set by the gradient noise scale (noise-to-signal ratio of the stochastic gradient). It can be estimated cheaply from the per-GPU gradients that data parallelism already computes. It also predicts efficiency at batch sizes not yet run, and it tends to grow during training, by up to 10× or more. → Design: goodput = throughput × statistical efficiency, with EFFICIENCY_t(M) = (φ_t + M0)/(φ_t + M), relative to the user's initial batch M0. Pollux uses a pre-conditioned version (PGNS) for Adam and AdaGrad. With one GPU it uses a differenced-variance estimator over consecutive gradients. → Removes the blind spot of throughput-only scaling: "many GPUs + big batch" and "few GPUs + small batch" are now compared on one scale, and the comparison changes as training goes on.
  2. Insight: throughput depends on only a few things the scheduler controls: GPU count, co-location, per-GPU batch and gradient accumulation. A small parametric model fitted online is accurate enough. → Design: a 7-parameter model of Titer: Tgrad linear in m; Tsync linear in K, with separate parameters for same-node and cross-node; a learned γ for compute/communication overlap; and accumulation steps s. It is fitted by RMSLE with L-BFGS-B. Priors assume perfect scaling until a configuration has been tried, so the scheduler explores. A job can get at most 2× the most GPUs it has held so far. → New jobs can be scheduled with no offline profiling.
  3. Insight: the value of an allocation is the best goodput the job can reach on it after re-tuning its batch, not its throughput at the current batch. → Design: a PolluxAgent runs with each job. It fits the goodput function, picks (m, s) to maximize goodput on the current allocation, scales the LR through a plug-in rule, and reports its fitted model (θ_sys, φ_t) every 30 s. PolluxSched runs every 60 s. It maximizes a power mean (exponent p) of each job's SPEEDUP, defined as best goodput on the candidate allocation ÷ best goodput on an exclusive 1/J share. p is the fairness knob, with p = −1 as default. The search is population-based. A reallocation penalty (T_j − R_j·δ)/(T_j + δ) is based on each job's past reallocation rate. At most one distributed job may run per node. → The cluster gives jobs more GPUs and big batches when idle, and fewer GPUs and small, efficient batches when busy. A throughput-only scheduler cannot make this trade.
- Evidence: Testbed of 16 AWS g4dn.12xlarge nodes (64 T4 GPUs), 160 jobs sampled from the busiest 8 hours of the Microsoft trace, 6 model types. Against baselines with expert-tuned configurations, average JCT is 0.76 h vs 1.5 h (Optimus+Oracle) and 1.2 h (Tiresias): 50% and 37% shorter. p99 is 27% shorter for both, and makespan 20% and 33% shorter. Against realistic user configurations (2.7 h, 2.8 h), average JCT is 72% and 73% shorter. In simulation: 48% and 32%. At p = −1, 99% of jobs have finish-time fairness ρ < 2; max-ρ improves 1.5× and 5.4×. Efficiency predictions match measurements across batch sizes. The throughput model's average error is at most 10% on 64 GPUs. Final accuracy is within ±1% across batch sizes (DeepSpeech2 ±4%). Costs: one reallocation per job every 7 min on average, 8% runtime overhead, 1 s of scheduler CPU per 60 s round. Seeding from offline data improves JCT by only 2–5% (short jobs). Without interference avoidance, JCT is 1.4× longer at 50% slowdown. Cloud auto-scaling for ImageNet: 25% cheaper than throughput-based scaling (Or et al.), 6% slower. HPO (TPE, CIFAR-10): 30% faster at the same accuracy.

Safe background (may be explained whenever asked): SGD, mini-batches, synchronous data parallelism, all-reduce and NCCL, gradient accumulation, Amdahl's law, LR scaling rules (linear, square-root, AdaScale), checkpoint-restart, Kubernetes, Tiresias (priority queues, fixed GPU count) and Optimus (throughput model, picks GPU count) as designs, finish-time fairness (Themis). "Statistical efficiency" is a standard ML term: the tutor may use it once the learner describes "progress per example", but must not introduce it first.

## Exercise 1: Problem finding — why do jobs take so long, and why don't the obvious fixes help?
### Brief
You run a shared 64-GPU cluster for a research group: 16 nodes with 4 NVIDIA T4 GPUs each, on Kubernetes, with checkpoints on CephFS. The scheduler works like Tiresias: a user submits a job with a GPU count, a batch size and a learning rate, and the job waits until that many GPUs are free and keeps them until it ends. Jobs are synchronous data-parallel PyTorch with NCCL all-reduce. The mix includes image classification, object detection, speech, BERT fine-tuning and a recommender. Iteration time is compute time, which shrinks with a smaller per-GPU batch, plus all-reduce time, which does not, and which grows when a job spans nodes. Final accuracy holds across batch sizes only if the LR is raised with the batch by a rule (AdaScale for SGD, square-root for Adam); one team's rule breaks beyond some batch size, and it asked for a hard cap. You replay 160 jobs from the busiest 8 hours of a public Microsoft trace, with the trace's GPU counts and each batch set to the one-GPU batch times the GPU count, which is how most users fill in the form.

1. The replay gives an average job completion time (JCT) of 2.8 h. Users say that finding a good GPU count and batch size for one job means measuring many configurations and fully training the model at several batch sizes, so almost nobody does it.
2. Last quarter you tested an elastic scheduler that picks each job's GPU count from a fitted model of its throughput, while users keep their own batch size. Even with each job's exact iteration count supplied ahead of time, the same replay gave 2.7 h.
3. On ResNet18/CIFAR-10 you trained at three batch sizes, retuning the LR for each. The larger batches processed more examples per second, but counted per example seen they fell behind early, and later in training they made progress at almost the same rate.
4. Users complain both ways: "I asked for 16 GPUs and waited hours in the queue" and "I asked for 2, and my job ran all night while GPUs sat idle." The infra lead pushes back on moving jobs as load changes: each move is a checkpoint-restart of 15–120 s.
5. Two multi-node jobs placed on the same node both ran slower. A published study of a production DL cluster reports slowdowns of up to 50% when distributed jobs compete for the network.

Tutor notes: Strawmen: only 2 is shown being tried (an elastic scheduler that sets GPU counts by throughput, with the batch fixed). The others appear only as constraints, and the learner should come up with them: 1 (tuning each job by hand is costly) kills "let users or experts tune every job"; the accuracy cap in the background kills "always use the largest batch"; the restart cost in 4 kills "move jobs whenever load changes". 1 says the configuration is costly to get right, so users do not. (The hand-tuned baseline result, 1.2 h, is kept out of the brief: it is the strong baseline that exercise 3's E-claim asks for.) 2 says changing the GPU count alone, by throughput, with the batch fixed, does not help. With the background (all-reduce time does not shrink), it implies extra GPUs need a larger batch. 3 is the core one: a larger batch gives more throughput but less progress per example, by an amount that changes with the stage of training. 4 says the right size changes with load, and the restart cost rules out reshuffling all the time. 5 is the distractor: it is real, and the paper handles it (one distributed job per node), but it is about placement, not about choosing resources and batch together. Do not explain why the catch-up in 3 happens; its cause (gradient noise) is part of exercise 2.
### Task
In 4–6 sentences: what fails, the root cause, the obvious fix someone would try first and why it fails, and which observations support this. Say which observation, if any, is unrelated.
### Hint
Observations 1 and 2 change different things. Which one moved the number, and what did the tuned jobs change that the elastic scheduler did not?
### Rubric
1. [P-cause] GPU count and batch size are coupled, and a larger batch makes less progress per example, so choosing either one alone, or by throughput, gives bad configurations. Yes: states both halves (extra GPUs pay off only with a larger batch; a larger batch is worth less per example, so throughput overstates big configurations). Partial: only one half, or "users pick bad settings" with no reason why.
2. [P-strawman] The obvious fixes and why they fail. Expert tuning per job is costly and still fixed at submission. "Always the largest batch" loses progress per example early and hits the accuracy cap. Moving jobs all the time costs 15–120 s per move. Yes: names at least two of the three fixes, each with why it fails. Partial: names one, or names fixes without the reason.
3. [P-signal] Observations 1 and 2 together show the gain comes from the configuration, not from the GPU count alone or the queue policy, and 5 is unrelated. Yes: contrasts 1 with 2 and sets aside 5 with a reason. Partial: does one of the two.
4. [P-goal] A goal a solution can be judged against: the cluster chooses each job's resources and batch size (and LR) together, and keeps re-choosing as load and training stage change, by real training progress, while keeping final accuracy and fairness across jobs. Yes: joint choice, over time, by progress, with what must be kept. Partial: "schedule better" or "tune automatically" without the joint choice or the time aspect.
### Paper's answer
In a shared GPU cluster, users set GPU count, batch size and LR at submission, but the three depend on each other. More GPUs only help with a larger batch, because sync time does not shrink. A larger batch makes less training progress per example, so throughput alone overstates the value of big configurations. The best trade-off also shifts with cluster load and with the stage of training. Users get it wrong, even expert settings are static, and elastic schedulers tune only the GPU count by throughput and leave the batch alone. The problem: let the cluster choose each job's resources and its batch size (and LR) together, all the time, by real training progress, without hurting final accuracy, and fairly across jobs.

## Exercise 2: Solution design — choose resources and batch together, by real progress
### Brief
The problem (from exercise 1): GPU count, batch size and LR depend on each other, their best values shift with load and with training stage, and the cluster must choose them together for every job, by real training progress, without hurting final accuracy, and fairly. Some facts about the setting:

1. At every step, each GPU computes its own gradient over its share of the batch. Only the average after all-reduce is used; the local gradients are then discarded.
2. Validation accuracy is known only near the end of a job. Fitting a convergence curve to predict the remaining iterations does not work reliably across models.
3. A job's training loop can change its per-GPU batch, gradient accumulation and LR at any step, with an LR scaling rule (AdaScale, square-root) to keep accuracy.
4. Jobs are new models submitted by users, and the cluster has no idle GPUs for offline profiling runs.
5. Jobs count progress in different units (images, sequences, user-item pairs). Some models, like ImageNet, scale to many GPUs; most do not.

Tutor notes: 1 is the core of the efficiency insight (the spread of the local gradients measures gradient noise for free). 2 rules out measuring progress by accuracy or by curve fitting. 3 makes the batch a knob the system can turn, which the joint decision needs. 4 points to an online throughput model, which is in "also tell". 5 points to a unit-free, fair objective, also in "also tell".
### Task
Describe your design: (a) the one fact about the problem or workload it exploits, (b) the mechanism, (c) why it is correct, including the failure case that most threatens it, (d) what it costs and when that cost is bad.
### Hint
Look at fact 1. If the GPUs' local gradients nearly agree, how much would doubling the batch improve the averaged gradient? And if they disagree a lot?
### Rubric
1. [S-insight] Real speed is throughput × progress per example, and progress per example at an untried batch size can be predicted from how much the per-example (per-GPU) gradients disagree, which the job already computes. Yes: the combined metric and a cheap online predictor based on gradient noise or variance. Partial: the combined metric, but progress per example found by trial runs, curve fitting or accuracy, or not predicted at all. A correct alternative (another estimate of the batch size beyond which extra examples stop helping, measured online from gradients) earns Yes.
2. [S-mechanism] The joint decision: the scheduler values each candidate allocation at the best useful progress the job can reach on it after re-tuning its batch (and LR), and the job then re-tunes on the GPUs it gets. Yes: says the allocation is priced assuming the best batch for it, and says what runs beside each job (gradient statistics, timing, batch and LR changes) and what runs centrally (the allocation). Partial: the scheduler picks GPUs by throughput and the job tunes its batch afterwards, or a joint search with no say on who computes what.
3. [S-correct] Final accuracy is kept, and the predictions work with little data. Yes: the LR is rescaled with the batch by a rule and the user's batch cap is respected, plus at least one estimation corner case (one GPU, so no spread across GPUs: use consecutive steps; or an allocation never tried: assume something optimistic so the scheduler explores). Partial: only the accuracy part, or only a corner case.
4. [S-cost] What the design pays. Yes: each reallocation is a 15–120 s restart, so moves must be penalized; and maximizing total progress lets a few scalable jobs take most GPUs, so the objective must be unit-free and fair. Partial: names one of the two. A correct alternative for fairness (normalize each job by its progress on a fair share, or bound finish-time fairness) earns Yes.
### Paper's answer
Pollux measures each configuration by goodput = throughput × statistical efficiency, relative to the user's initial batch M0. Efficiency at any batch M is predicted from the gradient noise scale φ: EFFICIENCY(M) = (φ + M0)/(φ + M). φ is estimated for free from the spread of the per-GPU gradients; with one GPU, from consecutive steps; for Adam and AdaGrad, a pre-conditioned version (PGNS) is used. φ tends to grow during training, by up to 10× or more, which explains why large batches catch up late, and why the best batch grows over a job's life. Pollux is co-adaptive: a PolluxAgent beside each job fits goodput online, picks per-GPU batch, gradient accumulation and LR (through a plug-in scaling rule) for its current GPUs, and reports its fitted model every 30 s. Every 60 s, PolluxSched values each candidate allocation at the job's best goodput on it, divided by its best goodput on an exclusive 1/J share (SPEEDUP), and maximizes a power mean of those speedups. So the cluster gives jobs more GPUs and big batches when it is idle, and fewer GPUs and small, efficient batches when it is busy.
### Also tell
- Throughput model: 7 parameters, with Tgrad linear in the per-GPU batch, Tsync linear in GPU count with separate same-node and cross-node terms, a learned overlap term γ, and accumulation steps. It is fitted online by RMSLE with L-BFGS-B. Priors assume perfect scaling until a configuration has been tried, so the scheduler explores; a job can get at most 2× the most GPUs it has held so far. No offline profiling.
- Cluster objective: the power-mean exponent p is the fairness knob (p = 1 is the sum; p → −∞ approaches the minimum; default p = −1). The search is population-based.
- Reallocation penalty (T_j − R_j·δ)/(T_j + δ), based on each job's past reallocation rate. At most one distributed job per node (the interference in exercise 1).
- The next exercise uses these names.
### Common gaps
- "Pick the GPU count that maximizes throughput" → With the batch fixed, sync time sets a floor on iteration time. Which observation in exercise 1 already tried this?
- "Grow the batch to maximize throughput" → Throughput counts examples, not progress. A throughput-driven auto-scaler scales out to a big batch at the start and stays there. Is the start of training when big batches are worth most?
- Combined metric, but found progress per example by running each batch size → That is the expert's full-training cost from exercise 1. What does the job compute at every step that tells you how much a bigger batch would help?
- Scheduler picks GPUs, then each job picks its own batch → When the scheduler prices 8 GPUs for a job, which batch size does it assume? What if the job would grow its batch on those GPUs?
- "Maximize total progress across jobs" → Jobs count images, sequences and pairs. What is the unit of the sum, and what stops ImageNet from taking most of the GPUs?
- Reallocates every round → Each move is 15–120 s. Which jobs should pay more for moving?

## Exercise 3: Experiment design — convince a reviewer
### Brief
Problem: in shared DL clusters, users fix GPU count, batch size and LR at submission. These are interdependent, and their best values shift with load and training progress. Existing schedulers either keep the user's choice (Tiresias) or tune GPU count by throughput only (Optimus). Method (Pollux): a job's goodput is throughput × statistical efficiency. Efficiency is predicted from the (pre-conditioned) gradient noise scale; throughput comes from a 7-parameter model fitted online. A PolluxAgent per job fits these models, chooses per-GPU batch, gradient accumulation and LR for the current allocation, and reports to PolluxSched. Every 60 s, PolluxSched maximizes a power mean (p = −1) of each job's goodput speedup over a 1/J fair share. It penalizes reallocations and keeps distributed jobs on separate nodes. The authors claim much shorter job completion times than existing schedulers, even when those get expert configurations, without loss of final accuracy, and with fairness across jobs.
### Task
List the 2–3 research questions the evaluation must answer. For the most important one, give the baselines, the workload, the metric, and the result that would refute the claim. For the others, one line each.
### Hint
Beating users who choose badly is easy. What must the baselines be given so that the comparison is fair?
### Rubric
1. [E-claim] JCT against strong schedulers that get expert configurations, not only user ones, on a realistic trace. Yes: a tuned-configuration setting for the baselines, plus a fair setup (same LR scaling rules for all, or the same throughput model), and a tail metric or makespan besides the mean. Partial: only against user configurations, only the mean, or baselines given worse support than Pollux.
2. [E-refute] The predictions and the accuracy constraint. Yes: predicted efficiency at untried batch sizes vs measured progress, or throughput-model error, and final accuracy across batch sizes, on several models. Partial: checks only throughput, only one model, or never checks final accuracy.
3. [E-ablation] Where the gain comes from: batch tuning vs the allocation search. Yes: an ablation that holds one part fixed (for example Pollux with the batch fixed, efficiency set to 1). Partial: shows allocations and batch sizes over time, or per job across load changes, without isolating the parts.
4. [E-cost] Who pays. Yes: a fairness metric across jobs (for example a finish-time fairness CDF, with a p sweep) and the reallocation overhead (moves per job, runtime lost). Partial: one of the two, or overhead with no number.
### Paper's answer
§5. (1) End-to-end JCT on 16 × g4dn.12xlarge (64 T4), with 160 jobs from the busiest 8 hours of the Microsoft trace, each mapped to one of 6 models of the same GPU-time class. Baselines: Tiresias (two priority queues, threshold hand-tuned) and Optimus+Oracle (given Pollux's throughput model and exact iteration counts); all use the same LR scaling rules. With expert-tuned baseline configurations (GPUs and batch chosen to reach 50–80% of ideal scaling), average JCT is 0.76 h vs 1.5 h and 1.2 h; p99 27% shorter, makespan 20% and 33% shorter. With user configurations: 2.7 h and 2.8 h for the baselines. In simulation on 8 traces: 48% and 32% shorter, and gains hold across job submission rates. (2) Efficiency predicted from φ at one batch size matches measurements over a range of batch sizes for all 6 models. The throughput model's average error is at most 10% on 64 GPUs. Final metrics are within ±1% across batch sizes (DeepSpeech2 ±4%). (3) Mechanism: cluster-wide allocated GPUs and average efficiency over time (Fig. 5); ImageNet and YOLOv3 followed through low, high and low load (Fig. 6), where ImageNet gets more GPUs and a bigger batch at low load, and a much larger per-GPU batch in the second quiet period. (4) At p = −1, 99% of jobs have ρ < 2; p = 1 has a long tail with ρ > 4. One reallocation per job every 7 min, 8% runtime overhead; scheduler 1 s per 60 s round on 1 vCPU; agent fit 0.2 s every 30 s. In simulation, intervals up to 2 min are fine and no interference avoidance makes JCT 1.4× longer at 50% slowdown. Where it falls short: no ablation separates batch tuning from the allocation search (Optimus differs in both); both baselines get help, none goes the other way; the trace has no model information, so jobs were mapped to 6 models; only homogeneous T4s, data parallelism and 64 GPUs, and the paper says its throughput model may not hold beyond that.
### Also tell
- Seeding the models from offline data gains only 2–5% JCT, on short jobs.
- Cloud auto-scaling for ImageNet: 25% cheaper than throughput-based scaling (Or et al.), 6% slower.
- Hyperparameter search (TPE, CIFAR-10): 30% faster at the same accuracy.
### Common gaps
- Compared only against user configurations → Observation 1 in exercise 1 shows tuning alone more than halves JCT. What does beating untuned users prove?
- Gave the baselines a worse throughput model or no LR scaling → Then the gain mixes in unrelated factors. Which help must every scheduler get?
- No ablation → Optimus differs from Pollux both in tuning the batch and in how it allocates. How would you show which one gives the gain?
- Never checked final accuracy → What happens on a task where large batches hurt generalization before efficiency drops? Is a user cap enough?
- No fairness metric → A goodput-maximizer can starve jobs that do not scale. Which metric would show that, and which knob trades it off?
- Simulator results only → How would you show that the simulator agrees with the testbed?
