# Pollux: Co-adaptive Cluster Scheduling for Goodput-Optimized Deep Learning
OSDI'21 (Best Paper) · Aurick Qiao, Sang Keun Choe, Suhas Jayaram Subramanya, Willie Neiswanger, Qirong Ho, Hao Zhang, Gregory R. Ganger, Eric P. Xing / Petuum, CMU, UC Berkeley, MBZUAI · https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi21/presentation/qiao
Paper type: new system (cluster scheduler for deep learning training)
> Spoiler: this card dissects the paper. Do not read it if you want to train on this paper.

## Dissection
- Background: Shared GPU clusters run many deep learning (DL) training jobs at once. Most jobs use synchronous data parallelism: each GPU computes a gradient on its share of the mini-batch, then all GPUs average the gradients (all-reduce) before each step. Iteration time is roughly compute time (Tgrad, shrinks with a smaller per-GPU batch) plus sync time (Tsync, does not shrink with batch size and grows when a job spans nodes). When the total batch grows, the learning rate (LR) must be scaled up by a rule (linear, square-root, AdaScale), or final accuracy suffers. Schedulers fall in two groups. Tiresias and Gandiva take the GPU count from the user and keep it fixed. Optimus, Gavel, AntMan and similar systems pick the GPU count from a model of each job's throughput.
- Problem: Users must choose GPU count, batch size and LR at submission. The three depend on each other: more GPUs only pay off with a larger batch, and a larger batch makes less training progress per example. The best choice also changes while the job runs, with cluster load and with the stage of training. Users choose badly, and even an expert choice is a static value. Schedulers that pick GPU counts look only at throughput and leave the batch alone. So no part of the system chooses resources and training parameters together, using a measure of real training progress.
- Challenges: (1) Progress per example at a batch size never tried is unknown, and final accuracy is known only at the end. Trying each batch size costs full training runs. (2) A throughput model must cover allocations the job has never run on, and it must be fitted online for models the scheduler has never seen. (3) What an allocation is worth to a job depends on how the job will re-tune its batch once it has the GPUs. So the scheduler and the job cannot decide separately. (4) Jobs measure progress in different units and want different amounts of GPUs, so the cluster-wide objective must be comparable across jobs and fair. (5) Each reallocation is a checkpoint-restart that costs 15–120 s. Two distributed jobs on one node slow each other down.
- Solution:
  1. Insight: how much progress per example a larger batch loses is set by the gradient noise scale (noise-to-signal ratio of the stochastic gradient). It can be estimated cheaply from the per-GPU gradients that data parallelism already computes. It also predicts efficiency at batch sizes not yet run, and it tends to grow during training, by up to 10× or more. → Design: goodput = throughput × statistical efficiency, with EFFICIENCY_t(M) = (φ_t + M0)/(φ_t + M), relative to the user's initial batch M0. Pollux uses a pre-conditioned version (PGNS) for Adam and AdaGrad. With one GPU it uses a differenced-variance estimator over consecutive gradients. → Removes the blind spot of throughput-only scaling: "many GPUs + big batch" and "few GPUs + small batch" are now compared on one scale, and the comparison changes as training goes on.
  2. Insight: throughput depends on only a few things the scheduler controls: GPU count, co-location, per-GPU batch and gradient accumulation. A small parametric model fitted online is accurate enough. → Design: a 7-parameter model of Titer: Tgrad linear in m; Tsync linear in K, with separate parameters for same-node and cross-node; a learned γ for compute/communication overlap; and accumulation steps s. It is fitted by RMSLE with L-BFGS-B. Priors assume perfect scaling until a configuration has been tried, so the scheduler explores. A job can get at most 2× the most GPUs it has held so far. → New jobs can be scheduled with no offline profiling.
  3. Insight: the value of an allocation is the best goodput the job can reach on it after re-tuning its batch, not its throughput at the current batch. → Design: a PolluxAgent runs with each job. It fits the goodput function, picks (m, s) to maximize goodput on the current allocation, scales the LR through a plug-in rule, and reports its fitted model (θ_sys, φ_t) every 30 s. PolluxSched runs every 60 s. It maximizes a power mean (exponent p) of each job's SPEEDUP, defined as best goodput on the candidate allocation ÷ best goodput on an exclusive 1/J share. p is the fairness knob, with p = −1 as default. The search is population-based. A reallocation penalty (T_j − R_j·δ)/(T_j + δ) is based on each job's past reallocation rate. At most one distributed job may run per node. → The cluster gives jobs more GPUs and big batches when idle, and fewer GPUs and small, efficient batches when busy. A throughput-only scheduler cannot make this trade.
- Evidence: Testbed of 16 AWS g4dn.12xlarge nodes (64 T4 GPUs), 160 jobs sampled from the busiest 8 hours of the Microsoft trace, 6 model types. Against baselines with expert-tuned configurations, average JCT is 0.76 h vs 1.5 h (Optimus+Oracle) and 1.2 h (Tiresias): 50% and 37% shorter. p99 is 27% shorter for both, and makespan 20% and 33% shorter. Against realistic user configurations (2.7 h, 2.8 h), average JCT is 72% and 73% shorter. In simulation: 48% and 32%. At p = −1, 99% of jobs have finish-time fairness ρ < 2; max-ρ improves 1.5× and 5.4×. Efficiency predictions match measurements across batch sizes. The throughput model's average error is at most 10% on 64 GPUs. Final accuracy is within ±1% across batch sizes (DeepSpeech2 ±4%). Costs: one reallocation per job every 7 min on average, 8% runtime overhead, 1 s of scheduler CPU per 60 s round. Seeding from offline data improves JCT by only 2–5% (short jobs). Without interference avoidance, JCT is 1.4× longer at 50% slowdown. Cloud auto-scaling for ImageNet: 25% cheaper than throughput-based scaling (Or et al.), 6% slower. HPO (TPE, CIFAR-10): 30% faster at the same accuracy.

## Stage 1: Find the problem
### Background to give
You run a shared 64-GPU cluster for a research group: 16 nodes, each with 4 NVIDIA T4 GPUs, on Kubernetes, with checkpoints on CephFS. The scheduler works like Tiresias. A user submits a job with a GPU count, a batch size and a learning rate. The job waits until that many GPUs are free and keeps them until it ends. Jobs are synchronous data-parallel PyTorch with NCCL all-reduce. The usual mix: ResNet-50 on ImageNet, YOLOv3 on PASCAL-VOC, DeepSpeech2 on CMU-ARCTIC, BERT fine-tuning on SQuAD, ResNet18 on CIFAR-10, and a NeuMF recommender on MovieLens. Most jobs are the small CIFAR-10 and recommender jobs (36% each). ImageNet jobs are rare (2%) but huge.

### Phenomena
1. You replayed 160 jobs from the busiest 8 hours of a public Microsoft trace on the cluster. GPU counts came from the trace. Each batch size was the job's one-GPU batch times its GPU count, which is how most users fill in the form. Average job completion time: 2.8 h. Then a colleague hand-tuned every job. They measured iteration time over many GPU counts and batch sizes, and fully trained each model at several batch sizes. Then they gave each job a GPU count that reaches 50–80% of linear scaling at its best batch size. Same scheduler, same jobs: 1.2 h. They say nobody will do that work for every job they submit.
2. A teammate added GPUs to a YOLOv3 job and kept the per-GPU batch the same. Time per iteration rose sharply as soon as the job went past 4 GPUs, the point where it had to span two nodes. Most of the models show this. ImageNet is the exception. Separately, with the GPU count fixed, examples per second kept rising as the total batch went up. For YOLOv3 and BERT it kept rising even past what fits in GPU memory, using gradient accumulation.
3. Two multi-node jobs were placed on the same node, and both ran slower. A published study of a production DL cluster reports slowdowns of up to 50% when distributed jobs compete for the network.
4. Last quarter you tested an elastic scheduler that picks each job's GPU count from a fitted model of its throughput. Users keep their own batch size. It needed each job's exact number of iterations to completion, so you ran every job ahead of time to supply it. Same trace, user configurations: 2.7 h average JCT, against 2.8 h before.
5. On ResNet18/CIFAR-10 you trained at three batch sizes and retuned the LR for each. Counted per training example seen, the larger batches fell behind early. Later in training they made progress at almost the same rate per example. ImageNet shows sudden jumps in this curve each time its LR schedule steps down. BERT fine-tuning and the recommender barely show the late catch-up. Final accuracy across batch sizes stayed within ±1% (±4% for DeepSpeech2), but only when the LR was raised with the batch (AdaScale for SGD, square-root for Adam). Without that, quality is significantly worse. One team found its LR rule breaks down beyond some batch size, and asked for a hard cap.
6. Complaints on the cluster channel go both ways. "I asked for 16 GPUs and waited hours in the queue." "I asked for 2 so I wouldn't wait, and now my job runs all night while GPUs sit idle." Someone proposes moving jobs to other GPUs as load changes. The infra lead replies that a move is a checkpoint-restart, which takes 15 to 120 s depending on the model and its start-up code.

Tutor notes: 1 says configuration matters a lot and is costly to get right (tuned vs. untuned under the same policy). 2 and 5 together are the core. More GPUs need a bigger batch (Tsync floor), and a bigger batch gives less progress per example, by an amount that changes with the stage of training. 4 says that adding GPUs while the batch stays fixed does not help. 6 says the right size changes with load. 5 (the accuracy limit and the cap) and 6 (restart cost) are the constraints: they rule out "always use the largest batch" and "reshuffle all the time". 3 is the distractor: it is real and the paper does handle it (one distributed job per node), but it is about placement, not about choosing resources and batch together. Do not explain why the catch-up in 5 happens. Its cause (gradient noise) is a stage-2 hint.

### Hint ladder
- L1: Phenomena 1 and 4 change different things. One changes who picks the configuration, the other changes who picks the GPU count. Which one moved the number, and what did the tuned configurations change that the elastic scheduler did not?
- L2: Put 2 and 5 side by side. What do you need to do to make extra GPUs useful, and what does that cost you per example? Is that cost the same at the start and at the end of a job?
- L3: Suppose a scheduler sees only examples per second. In which of 2, 5 and 6 would it make the wrong call? What would it need to know, and what would it need to control, to make the right one?
- Bottom-out: state the target below.

### Target
In a shared GPU cluster, GPU count, batch size and LR are set by users at submission, but they depend on each other. More GPUs only help with a larger batch, and a larger batch makes less training progress per example. The best trade-off also shifts with cluster load and with training progress. Users get it wrong, even expert settings are static, and elastic schedulers tune only the GPU count by throughput, blind to progress per example. The problem: let the cluster choose each job's resources and its batch size (and LR) together, all the time, by real training progress, without hurting final accuracy, and fairly across jobs.
Checklist (the stage ends when the core items are covered in the learner's own words):
- [core] GPU count and batch size are coupled: extra GPUs need a larger batch to be useful (sync time does not shrink).
- [core] A larger batch makes less progress per example, so throughput alone overstates the value of big configurations.
- [core] The best choice changes over time, with cluster load and with training stage, so a choice fixed at submission is wrong.
- [core] Current schedulers either take the user's choice or tune GPU count by throughput only; none of them tunes the batch.
- [detail] Constraints: final accuracy (LR rescaling, batch caps), reallocation cost, fairness between jobs.

### Common wrong turns
- "Give users a better tuning guide" → Phenomenon 1 shows even the expert's settings are fixed at submission. What does phenomenon 6 do to a setting that was right at 3 a.m.?
- "Make the scheduler elastic in GPU count" → That is phenomenon 4: 2.8 h to 2.7 h. What did it leave unchanged that the tuned jobs changed?
- "Always use the largest batch that fits, with gradient accumulation" → Phenomenon 5: early in training, how much is each of those examples worth? And what does the team with the cap say?
- "It's a queueing policy problem: better priorities, shortest job first" → In phenomenon 1 the policy did not change and JCT more than halved. What did change?
- "Interference between jobs on a node" → Phenomenon 3 explains slowdowns on shared nodes. Does it explain the gap between 2.8 h and 1.2 h?

## Stage 2: Derive the solution
### Seed hint
If the scheduler had to compare "8 GPUs at a big batch" with "2 GPUs at a small batch" for the same job, what one number would it compare? And what would it need to measure, while the job runs, to compute that number for a configuration the job has never run?

### Path
1. Learner may first say "pick the GPU count that maximizes throughput". Wall: phenomenon 4 and Amdahl. With the batch fixed, Tsync sets a floor on iteration time, so extra GPUs stop paying off. This is Optimus, and it barely beats Tiresias.
2. Learner says "then also grow the batch, to maximize throughput". Wall: phenomenon 5. Throughput counts examples, not progress. A throughput-driven auto-scaler (Or et al.) scales out to many nodes and a big batch at the start and stays there, which is exactly when big batches are least efficient. Ask: how would you discount throughput by what each example is worth?
3. Learner proposes "throughput × progress per example". Wall: how do you know progress per example at a batch size you have not run? Validation accuracy comes only at the end. Fitting a convergence curve to predict iterations left (what Optimus does) does not work reliably across models; the paper had to give Optimus an oracle. Running each batch size is the expert's full-training cost from phenomenon 1. Ask: what per-step quantity, which the job already computes, tells you how much a bigger batch would help the gradient estimate?
4. With hints, learner reaches gradient noise. If the per-example gradients are noisy, a bigger batch still improves the estimate; if they agree, extra examples add little. Each GPU's local gradient is a sample, and the spread across GPUs measures the noise at no extra cost. A simple formula, (φ + M0)/(φ + M), then predicts efficiency at any M. And φ tends to grow during training, which explains the late catch-up in phenomenon 5. Corner: with one GPU there is no spread across GPUs; use consecutive steps instead.
5. Throughput side. Learner may propose profiling each job offline. Wall: jobs are new models submitted by users, and the cluster has no idle GPUs for profiling runs. Fit a small model online instead. Wall: at the start there is no data for allocations the job has not tried. Ask: what assumption makes the scheduler try more GPUs instead of never trying? (Assume perfect scaling until measured, and cap growth at 2× the most GPUs held so far.)
6. Learner splits the work: the scheduler picks GPUs by throughput, and each job then picks its own batch. Wall: the scheduler undervalues giving GPUs to a job that would grow its batch to use them, so the two sides work against each other. Local adaptive-batch methods (AdaBatch, CABS) assume extra GPUs are there whenever a bigger batch wants them, which is rarely true in a shared cluster. Ask: when the scheduler prices an allocation, which batch size should it assume? (The best one for that allocation.)
7. Learner proposes "maximize total goodput". Wall: jobs measure examples in different units (images, sequences, pairs), and the sum lets a few scalable jobs (ImageNet) take most of the GPUs. Ask: what is a unit-free value, and how do you trade the total against equality? (Speedup over an exclusive 1/J share; a power mean whose exponent moves from sum (p = 1) toward min (p → −∞).)
8. Learner proposes reallocating every round. Wall: phenomenon 6, 15–120 s per checkpoint-restart. Penalize moves, and penalize more for jobs that have moved often. Also: do not put two multi-node jobs on one node (phenomenon 3).
Also discuss: which parts must run near the job (gradient statistics, iteration timing, batch and LR changes), and which must be central (the allocation)? That split gives a per-job agent that reports a small goodput model to a central scheduler.

### Hint ladder
Idea 1 (one metric for useful progress):
- L1: Examples per second counts work done. What is the work for?
- L2: In phenomenon 5, if a big batch needs twice as many examples to reach the same accuracy, what is its real speed?
- L3: Write "real speed" as a product of two things, one from the system and one from the training math.
- Bottom-out: useful progress per second = throughput × progress per example (relative to the user's batch). Compare all configurations on that.
Idea 2 (predict progress per example cheaply):
- L1: What does the job compute at every step, on every GPU, that you are currently throwing away after averaging?
- L2: If the GPUs' local gradients nearly agree, how much would doubling the batch improve the averaged gradient? And if they disagree a lot?
- L3: The ratio of gradient variance to squared mean gradient is one number. How would you turn it into "how many more examples does batch M need than batch M0 for the same progress"?
- Bottom-out: the gradient noise scale φ, estimated from per-GPU gradients; efficiency(M) = (φ + M0)/(φ + M); φ grows during training, so the best batch grows too. Use a pre-conditioned version for Adam, and consecutive steps when there is one GPU.
Idea 3 (price allocations with the job's re-tuning in mind):
- L1: When the scheduler asks "what is 8 GPUs worth to job A?", what batch size is it assuming?
- L2: If job A would change its batch on 8 GPUs, whose number should the scheduler use: A's current throughput, or something else?
- L3: The job can compute "best goodput over batch sizes on allocation a" for any a, if it sends the scheduler its fitted model. What should the scheduler maximize across jobs so that units cancel out and no job starves?
- Bottom-out: each job's agent fits throughput and noise online and picks its batch and LR for its current GPUs. The scheduler values each candidate allocation at the job's best goodput on it, ÷ goodput on a 1/J fair share. It maximizes a power mean of those speedups (p as the fairness knob), with a penalty for reallocations and at most one distributed job per node.

### Target
1. Measure configurations by useful progress per second: throughput × statistical efficiency (relative to the user's initial batch). That removes the bias toward big batches that throughput-only scaling has.
2. Predict efficiency at untried batch sizes from the gradient noise scale, estimated for free from per-GPU gradients. Its growth during training makes larger batches worth it later. Predict throughput from a small parametric model fitted online, with priors of perfect scaling to drive exploration.
3. Value an allocation at the best goodput the job can reach on it after re-tuning batch and LR. A per-job agent tunes and reports; a central scheduler maximizes a power mean of speedups over a fair share, with reallocation penalties and interference avoidance.
Checklist:
- [core] A combined metric of throughput and progress per example.
- [core] A cheap, online way to predict progress per example at unseen batch sizes (gradient noise), and why it changes over training.
- [core] Joint decision: the scheduler prices allocations assuming the job re-tunes its batch on them.
- [detail] Online throughput model with priors that encourage exploration.
- [detail] Unit-free, fair cluster objective (speedup over fair share, power mean, p).
- [detail] Reallocation penalty; one distributed job per node; LR rule as a plug-in; user batch cap respected.

## Stage 3: Design the experiments
### Summary to give
Problem: in shared DL clusters, users fix GPU count, batch size and LR at submission. These are interdependent, and their best values shift with load and training progress. Existing schedulers either keep the user's choice (Tiresias) or tune GPU count by throughput only (Optimus). Method (Pollux): define a job's goodput as throughput × statistical efficiency. Efficiency is predicted from the (pre-conditioned) gradient noise scale; throughput comes from a 7-parameter model fitted online. A PolluxAgent per job fits these models, chooses per-GPU batch, gradient accumulation and LR for the current allocation, and reports to PolluxSched. Every 60 s, PolluxSched maximizes a power mean (p = −1) of each job's goodput speedup over a 1/J fair share. It penalizes reallocations and keeps distributed jobs on separate nodes.

### Key research questions
1. Does co-adapting resources and batch size cut JCT against strong schedulers, even when those get expert configurations? This is the headline. A reviewer will insist on the tuned case, because beating badly configured users is easy.
2. Is the goodput model accurate? Do efficiency predictions match measured progress at unseen batch sizes, does the throughput model fit, and is final accuracy preserved? Everything rests on these predictions.
3. Does the system actually make the claimed trade, more GPUs and big batches when idle and fewer GPUs and small batches when busy, and is that where the gain comes from? This is the mechanism check for idea 3.
4. What does it cost? Fairness across jobs, reallocation overhead, and sensitivity to the scheduling interval, the exploration priors and interference. The design moves jobs around and favors scalable ones, and a reviewer will ask who pays.

### Key experiments
1. End-to-end JCT. Testbed: 16 × g4dn.12xlarge (64 T4), 160 jobs from the busiest 8 hours (hours 3–10) of the Microsoft trace, each mapped to a Table 1 model of the same GPU-time class. Baselines: Tiresias (two priority queues, threshold hand-tuned) and Optimus+Oracle (given Pollux's throughput model and exact iteration counts). All three use the same LR scaling rules. Two baseline settings: TunedJobs (GPUs and batch chosen to reach 50–80% of ideal scaling) and realistic (trace GPU counts, batch = M0 × GPUs). Metrics: average JCT, p99 JCT, makespan. Result: 0.76 h vs 1.5 h and 1.2 h (tuned); vs 2.7 h and 2.8 h (realistic); p99 27% shorter, makespan 20% and 33% shorter (tuned). Simulator on 8 traces: 48% and 32%. Weak designs: comparing only against user configurations; giving the baselines a worse throughput model or no LR scaling, so the gain mixes in unrelated factors; reporting only the mean; a single load level (the paper sweeps job submission rate in simulation, and the relative gains hold).
2. Model accuracy. Efficiency: for each of the 6 models, φ measured at one batch size predicts efficiency over a range of batch sizes; validation curves plotted against "statistical epochs" line up across batch sizes; final metrics within ±1% (DeepSpeech2 ±4%). Throughput: fit over many placements and batch sizes on 64 GPUs, average error ≤ 10%. Weak designs: checking throughput only; testing one model; never checking final accuracy, which is the constraint users care about most.
3. Mechanism. Plot cluster-wide allocated GPUs and average efficiency over time for all three schedulers (Fig. 5). Follow single jobs (ImageNet, YOLOv3) through low → high → low contention (Fig. 6): ImageNet gets more GPUs and a bigger batch at low load, fewer at high load. When load drops again late in training, it uses a much larger per-GPU batch than in the first quiet period. Weak design: only end-to-end numbers. Also note the paper's own gap: no ablation isolates batch tuning from the allocation algorithm. Optimus differs in both. A clean ablation would run Pollux with the batch fixed, which the system already supports (efficiency fixed to 1).
4. Costs and fairness. Finish-time fairness ρ CDF: at p = −1, 99% of jobs have ρ < 2; p = 1 has a long tail with ρ > 4. The p sweep (+1, −1, −10) shows the average-vs-tail trade-off. Overheads: one reallocation per job every 7 min, 8% runtime. Scheduler: 1 s per 60 s round on 1 vCPU. Agent fit: 0.2 s every 30 s. Sensitivity in simulation: intervals up to 2 min are fine; offline seeding gains only 2–5% on short jobs; without interference avoidance, JCT is 1.4× longer at 50% slowdown. Weak designs: no fairness metric (a goodput-maximizer can starve unscalable jobs); hiding restart cost; a simulator not checked against the testbed (here simulator and testbed gains agree).

### Reviewer probes
- Both baselines get help (expert configs, an oracle for iteration counts). Does any help go the other way? Is the 50–80% scaling band for "tuned" a fair stand-in for experts?
- Which part of the gain comes from batch tuning, and which from the allocation search? How would you separate them?
- The Microsoft trace has no model information, so jobs were mapped to 6 models by GPU-time. How would the result change with a mix of large, poorly scaling models?
- Final accuracy is within ±1% for these 6 tasks. What happens on a task where large batches hurt generalization before efficiency drops? Is a user cap enough?
- 64 T4s on one placement group, all data-parallel. What changes with heterogeneous GPUs, model parallelism or thousands of GPUs, where the paper says its throughput model may not hold?

## Stage 4: Apply and extend
### Scenarios
1. Mixed GPU types. Setup: the cluster now has V100s, T4s and A100s, and the throughput model assumes identical GPUs (the paper says so in a footnote). Question: what has to change in the goodput model and in the allocation search? Does the "best batch for an allocation" idea still hold when one job spans GPU types? Good directions: make throughput per accelerator type, as Gavel does, so speedups stay comparable. Forbid mixed-type jobs, or model the slowest GPU as the bottleneck of synchronous steps. Note that efficiency depends only on batch size, so that half carries over unchanged.
2. Hyperparameter search with early stopping. Setup: a bandit-style tuner (Hyperband-like) launches 64 trials at once and kills most after a few epochs. The paper tests only a Bayesian tuner with 4 concurrent trials and leaves this case open. Question: is "maximize each job's goodput" the right goal when most jobs will be killed soon? Good directions: early trials are in their low-efficiency phase, so give them small batches and few GPUs, and give GPUs to survivors as their efficiency rises. Let the tuner report each trial's expected remaining life, so the reallocation penalty and the fair share can use it. Watch out: a batch size that changes during a trial may confound the comparison between trials.
3. Move the insight to another system. Question: name another system where the obvious throughput metric overcounts useful work, and where a cheap online signal can predict the useful fraction for settings not yet tried. Good directions: LLM serving, where tokens/s at a large batch can miss latency targets; useful throughput there = requests meeting their SLO per second, and the scheduler picks batch size and GPUs together. Speculative decoding, where the acceptance rate discounts drafted tokens. Data-loading or compression pipelines, where a cheap sample predicts the payoff. A good answer names the discount factor, how to measure it online, and the knob it couples to resource choice.
